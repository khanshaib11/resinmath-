How to calculate epoxy for a river table

A river is basically a long, irregular box. Work out the volume of that box, convert it to liquid units, then adjust for reality.

Volume (in³) = Length × Average width × Depth
US gallons = in³ ÷ 231  ·  US fl oz = in³ ÷ 1.805
Liters = (L × W × D in cm) ÷ 1000

Worked example

A 72" long river, averaging 8" wide and 1.5" deep:

  • 72 × 8 × 1.5 = 864 in³
  • 864 ÷ 231 = 3.74 gallons (14.2 L)
  • +10% waste ≈ 4.1 gallons to buy

Measuring an irregular live edge

Rivers are rarely straight. Lay a tape across the gap every 4–6 inches along its whole length, write down each width, and average them. The more measurements you take, the more accurate the result.

For a glass-like finish, many makers fill the river with deep-pour resin, sand it flat, then add a thin flood coat of tabletop epoxy. You can work that coat out with the coating calculator.

Frequently asked questions

How much epoxy do I need for a river table?

Multiply river length × average width × depth in inches, divide by 231 to get US gallons, then add 10–15% for waste. A 72 × 8 × 1.5 inch river needs about 3.7 gallons, or roughly 4.1 gallons with 10% extra.

How do I measure the average width of an irregular river?

Measure the width every 4–6 inches along the river, add the measurements together and divide by how many you took.

How deep can I pour epoxy in one layer?

It depends on the product. Tabletop epoxies are usually limited to about ⅛–¼ inch, while deep-pour casting resins allow 1–4 inches per layer. Pouring too thick can overheat the resin, causing cracks, yellowing or even smoke. Always check the label.

Should I seal the wood before pouring?

Yes. A thin seal coat on the live edges stops air escaping from the wood into your pour as bubbles. Budget about 3–5% extra resin for sealing; the default waste allowance already covers this.
